## Free Will

[Free will](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_will) is the old question of whether we truly choose. Can we pick among options, control our behavior, and start actions, rather than just be pushed by earlier causes? [Sam Harris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sam_Harris) argued in [Free Will](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_Will_(book)) that it is an illusion. Decisions, he says, are fully caused by brain processes and background factors outside conscious control.

[Daniel Dennett](dennett.md), the leading defender of [compatibilism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compatibilism), answered in a [review](https://www.naturalism.org/resources/book-reviews/reflections-on-free-will). Free will, he said, means control, responding to reasons, and self-regulation, not a power that escapes cause and effect. Harris [replied](https://www.samharris.org/blog/the-marionettes-lament) that this changes the subject and dodges the question of ultimate authorship. I keep coming back to this exchange.

I found the tone of [Dennett's side](https://www.naturalism.org/resources/book-reviews/reflections-on-free-will) disappointing. It is one reason the [Discourse Protocol](../society/discourse.md) exists. Disagreement is certain, and progress depends on how it is handled.

On the substance, three layers help. The metaphysical question is whether choices are fixed by earlier states, including brain events. The psychological question is how deliberation actually happens. The normative question is whether people whose choices are caused should still be held accountable.

Much of the debate turns on [Benjamin Libet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Benjamin_Libet)'s experiments. There, a readiness signal in the brain comes before a voluntary act. Harris reads that as decisions being made before we know it. Later modeling by Aaron Schurger and colleagues, summarized under the [neuroscience of free will](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neuroscience_of_free_will), shows the signal is not necessarily the cause. Dennett adds that the conscious author of an act needs no access to how the brain produces it. A sailor steers without controlling wind or current.

The real split is about degrees. Dennett treats freedom as more or less control. Harris treats it as all or nothing and [concludes](https://www.samharris.org/blog/the-marionettes-lament) that no one controls anything. One side means the ability to do otherwise under identical conditions. The other means being guided by one's own reasons. Positions that fit together then look opposed.

Defining terms, and separating the empirical from the conceptual, dissolves much of the fight. That matters because responsibility, law, and how we see ourselves all rest on it. My open question: is the demand for ultimate authorship a fair requirement or a mistake about concepts?
