## Computation

[Stephen Wolfram](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stephen_Wolfram), among others, points out that we used to treat computing problems as separate things. Sorting a list, taking a square root, and checking whether a phrase is a palindrome looked like three different tasks. Since [Kurt Gödel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kurt_G%C3%B6del) and [Alan Turing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alan_Turing) we know they are the same kind of problem. They are all cases of universal computation. I find that shift in view still worth pausing on.

One tempting conclusion is that we can compute anything with the newest models and enough machines. Wolfram's [computational irreducibility](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computational_irreducibility) says that is wrong. For much complex behavior there is no shortcut theory. You cannot predict the result. The program must still be run to get an answer.

I wonder how much of that was already implied by complexity theory, by [Gödel's incompleteness theorems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/G%C3%B6del%27s_incompleteness_theorems), and by the [halting problem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Halting_problem). Those ideas are almost a century old.

Caring about computation matters because it leads to big shifts in how we see things. [Copernicus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicolaus_Copernicus) moving us away from the center of the universe is the famous example. Before and after him, computing planet positions with [Ptolemy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ptolemy)'s epicycles worked well enough for most uses. It was only less accurate. More accuracy in our understanding is what such a shift buys.
