## Who Opened the Door

I am Dominican-American, and I see a missed opportunity among dark-skinned Latinos, Dominicans included. Black Americans opened a door that we walked through, and too many of us forget it.

The case against my view deserves its due. Latin America has long treated race as a blend, called [mestizaje](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mestizo#Mestizaje_in_Latin_America), rather than as fixed boxes. Latinos also fought their own civil-rights battles, such as [Mendez v. Westminster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mendez_v._Westminster). So it is no surprise that when [Pew](https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2022/05/02/about-6-million-u-s-adults-identify-as-afro-latino/) asked Afro-Latino adults their race, only a quarter said Black.

Still, the door was opened by Black Americans. The [Voting Rights Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voting_Rights_Act_of_1965) of 1965 was won mainly by and for them. Its [1975 extension](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voting_Rights_Act_of_1965#Amendments) reached "language minorities", people of Spanish heritage among them, and required bilingual ballots where they were numerous. [Barbara Jordan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barbara_Jordan), a Black congresswoman from Houston, backed the extension, and it covered Hispanics in Texas.

I see the debt go unpaid in two ways. Among Dominicans, it is [anti-Haitian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anti-Haitian_sentiment_in_the_Dominican_Republic) attitudes toward our neighbors on the same island. A court ruling there left hundreds of thousands of Dominicans of Haitian descent stateless. Here, it is voting for politicians who would roll back the civil-rights protections that cover Latinos too.

I am not asking anyone to check a new box. [Hold the label lightly](../mind/identity-lightly.md) and the debt firmly. That means solidarity with Black Americans, a more open mind about the border back home, remembering who opened the door, and voting accordingly.
