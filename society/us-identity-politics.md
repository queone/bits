## U.S. Identity Politics

[Identity politics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Identity_politics) is not a recent infection, as I read it. Identity has always organized the U.S. party system. What changed is visibility and [polarization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Political_polarization_in_the_United_States).

The Democratic coalition gathers minority status, city life, higher education, and cosmopolitan values, held together by a shared self-image of inclusion. The Republican coalition gathers cultural continuity, national belonging, religion, and a felt loss of status. Its markers are appeals to "real Americans" and "traditional values." Partisan sorting has squeezed many identities into one label, so [disagreement lands as personal offense](trans-issues.md). Social media pours gasoline on this by rewarding outrage and certainty and punishing nuance. The mechanism is in the [monetization model](monetization-model.md).

U.S. politics is identity-first, with policy reasoned out afterward. Parties sell moral stories to different kinds of people. Voters buy the one that flatters their self-image and their fears. Once identity is engaged, reason becomes a lawyer, not a judge.

My impression of [Donald Trump](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Donald_Trump)'s supporters, not survey data, is that they want rid of what they see harming the country. They fear [immigration](immigration-choice.md) and the loss of their rights, and they see the opposition as entirely corrupt. That caricature has consequences.

The exit is a centrist coalition that rejects tribal loyalty as a virtue. It borrows working ideas from both sides and treats disagreement as information rather than heresy. As an individual, opt out. Refuse to [signal](virtue-signaling.md), caricature, or dehumanize, since every act of performative outrage feeds a machine that is paid to keep the temperature up.
