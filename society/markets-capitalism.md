## Markets and Capitalism

Markets are systems for coordinating people, and I try to judge them as such. They reward innovation. They pass information along through [price signals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Price_signal). In many settings they allocate resources faster than [economic planning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economic_planning). They coordinate huge numbers of people without anyone holding all the information, and they expand choice and room to specialize. They also fail in known ways. Prices ignore [externalities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Externality) such as pollution and long-term public-health costs. Outcomes concentrate wealth and [bargaining power](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bargaining_power) even while total output rises. Short-term incentives crowd out resilience and upkeep. And some goods distort when treated as products: attention, care, education, and political influence.

The question I find useful is never "markets or no markets". It is under what rules, incentives, and institutions markets produce outcomes worth wanting. Abstract models are incomplete. Real outcomes depend on human nature, institutional limits, cultural norms, and strategic behavior. That is why [game theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Game_theory) is part of the engine rather than a footnote. So instead of asking whether [capitalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capitalism) is good or bad, ask this. What incentives are being created? [Who captures the upside?](oligarchy.md) Who absorbs the downside? Which failures can markets not price honestly? What rules would align private gain with public benefit?

Markets are tools, not moral oracles. They drive progress only under conditions. With weak incentives, regulation, and institutions they amplify extraction, inequality, and short-term thinking just as easily.
